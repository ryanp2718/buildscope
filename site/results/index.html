Language models writing web scrapers

The price of a working program

We asked 19 language models to write the same scraper for a county permit portal, again and again, and checked every attempt against a hand-written one. Price per token turned out to be a poor guide to what a working program costs.

Protocol v2 model comparison, run 28-30 September 2026.

Each attempt, or draw, is one request: the model gets a sample of a portal's search results page and must write a Python extractor that returns every permit on it as rows. The extractor then runs on every saved page from that portal and passes only if its rows agree with a hand-written reference on all of them. The share of draws that pass is the model's pass@1.

The first portal, Clark County, Nevada, has 57 saved pages, and an extractor has to get every one of them right. How the comparison was kept fair across labs and hosts is at the end.

Paying more per token doesn't buy a working program

Clark County: pass@1 against output price

Each dot is one model. Bars are 95% confidence intervals.

List prices on the roster span about 140 times, from $0.14 to $20 per million output tokens. Price runs left to right; pass@1 runs bottom to top.

Each bar is a 95% confidence interval for the model's true pass rate. Ten draws pin a rate down only loosely, so a bar can span half the chart; the Wilson method keeps it honest at 0 of 10 and 10 of 10, where the textbook formula collapses to a point.

The best records are at the cheap end. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash passed 10 of 10 at $0.29 per million output tokens. Claude Haiku 4.5, at $5, passed 0 of 10.

What a team pays for is a working program. Divide what a model's draws cost by the number that passed, and the vertical axis becomes cost per success. The dashed lines show what fixed numbers of output tokens cost at each price.

Models with no passing draw have no cost per success, only a bill. They sit on the shelf at the top instead of being dropped from the chart.

Every draw is a square

A rate hides how it was earned. Here each square is one draw, on three portals: Clark County, where the prompt was developed, and St. Johns County, Florida, and Santa Barbara County, California, which no prompt was written against. On Clark, a model that passed or failed all of its first ten draws stopped there; the rest drew ten more, shown as a second row.

The red squares matter most. A red extractor ran without error but returned no rows on some page (full red) or rows that were wrong or missing (light red), the failure a pipeline doesn't notice until someone checks the numbers.

Many grey squares share one cause, and the prompt is part of it. It asks for a function extract(html), asks for entities such as &amp; to be decoded, and allows Python's html module, which decodes them. Inside the function the name html means the page, so an extractor that calls the module there stops with an error. That is 44 of the 158 grey squares, from 8 models, among them 7 of GPT-6 Sol's 10 on Clark. With only that name fixed, 25 of the 44 pass; the full report re-scores them.

Most of the extractors that returned nothing on Clark County share one cause. Clark's results grid carries a small table inside its first row: the bar that reads "Showing 1-10 of 100+".

An extractor that finds the grid and reads up to the first closing table tag stops inside that bar, before any permit. It returns nothing, and raises no error.

Take that one small table out of the saved pages and run the same extractors again.

Most of them now find the permits.

Instructions and settings barely moved the needle

Two changes were tested. A one-paragraph hint about Clark's row layout, written from the failures, was added to the prompt; and the seven models from an earlier test were re-run with this run's fairer settings. Each arrow runs from before to after. A blue arrow is a change the data can tell from chance; a grey one is not.

Show the data

With ten draws a side, only a difference of about 60 points or more is reliably detectable, so a grey arrow means "not shown", not "no effect".

Drawing again did

What did move it needs no new prompt: draw again, and keep the first extractor that passes a check needing no answer key. The check runs the extractor on the portal's saved pages and asks only what the pages themselves can answer: as many rows as the grid shows, a unique permit number on every row, no stray markup, dates that look like dates.

Allowed up to five draws, a model that passes some of the time ends with a working extractor far more often: 32 of 39 such model and portal pairs reach 95% or better, against a median of 60% from one draw. A working extractor costs about the same, a median 0.95 times a single draw's cost per success, because most runs stop at the first or second draw. Where a model never passed, there is nothing to pick.

Show the data

The check was written after the run, from the development portals: its row count rule was fixed on Clark and St. Johns before Santa Barbara was looked at. It was tried here on the extractors already bought, in 2,000 random orders of each pair's draws, so no new draws were made. Pairs with fewer than five draws are left out.

How the test was kept fair

Every model got the same prompt, a 64,000-token output budget, its lab's default reasoning level and its model card's sampling settings, and was served only by hosts running the weights at the lab's own precision. A draw cut off at the budget was re-drawn once at a higher cap (35 draws), so a long answer counts as a cost rather than a failure.

Failures of the host are not the model's. Some hosts close long streams at fixed times, and an early version of the harness scored those half-finished answers as the model's failures. Now such a call is retried, and a draw whose host fails twice is left out of the rate rather than counted against the model.

Scope and next steps

  • More portals. Three portals, one of them held out at five draws per model, already rank models differently. Two more held-out portals, Polk County and Oregon's statewide system, are collected and checked, and kept unused for the next test.
  • Letting the model run its program. Every draw here is one attempt with no feedback, so one slip, like the html name above, ends it. The check above picks a working extractor out of five draws almost as well as an oracle; the next experiment gives a model tools to run and repair its own extractor, and compares it with that at the same spend.
  • Every field, not just every record. A pass here means the right permits came out. On St. Johns, 39 of 160 passing extractors got some field wrong, most often by leaving a whole column empty; field-level pass rates are reported beside pass@1 in the full report.
  • Effort and the flagships. Each model ran at its lab's default reasoning level. An effort sweep, and the two most expensive flagship models left out for cost, are the obvious additions.

Pass@1 is agreement with a hand-written reference extractor on every saved page of a portal, not accuracy against the portal itself. Costs are what each draw cost to buy, in US dollars; prices are list prices. Intervals are 95% Wilson score intervals; cost-per-success ranges combine a Beta posterior on the pass rate with a bootstrap of the cost per draw.
